In the movie, Tony Stark taps his chest and tiny particles flow out, covering his whole body in armor within seconds. Could we really build an Iron Man nanosuit like this?
First we'll look at how the movie describes it, then split the suit into five pieces and see how far real labs have come with each one.
The first complete nanosuit appeared in the 2018 movie Avengers: Infinity War, as the Mark 50.
In the movie, nanoparticles pour out of the arc reactor in his chest to wrap his body, repair themselves when damaged, and even form thrusters that reach space.
The idea comes from the comics. In 2010, the Bleeding Edge armor was stored inside Tony's body and appeared with a single thought.
The visual effects company said it showed the suit being built by individual nanobots moving over his body, and refined it with Marvel for about two years.
It even has a fun weakness. Particles knocked off in a fight can't come back, so the supply keeps shrinking. It's matter, not endless magic.
To make this suit real, we need five things: tiny robots that assemble themselves, materials that change shape, the ability to heal itself, energy to power it all, and the human body inside.
A nanometer is one billionth of a meter. Despite the name, there is still no technology that gathers trillions of robots that small into a suit.
First, self-assembling robots. In 2014, Harvard researchers made 1,024 coin-sized robots signal each other and gather into stars and letters.
But they moved bit by bit on vibrating legs, and one shape took about 12 hours. A long way from the movie's one second.
MIT's cube robots have no wheels outside. A flywheel inside spins up to 20,000 times a minute and then stops suddenly, making the cube roll and jump and stick to others with magnets.
In 2007, DARPA, the U.S. defense research agency, began research on programmable matter, material that gathers into objects on command and then scatters again. It's the dream closest to a nanosuit.
In 2023, a striking shape-changing experiment appeared. A tiny human-shaped robot made of gallium, a metal that melts at 29.8 degrees Celsius, mixed with magnetic particles, melted under a magnetic field, slipped through prison bars, and solidified back into shape.
There are even smaller robots. In 2020, Cornell and the University of Pennsylvania built walking robots less than 0.1 millimeters long, moving their legs when a laser hits their solar cells.
A tiny self-moving robot announced in 2025 gets 75 nanowatts from light, less than one hundred-thousandth of what a smartwatch uses. The smaller the robot, the less power it can get.
There are also self-healing materials. In a plastic reported in 2001, cracks broke tiny capsules inside, releasing a healing agent that hardened and restored up to about 75 percent of the original strength.
At the smallest scale, there are machines built from individual molecules. The three scientists who designed and built molecular machines won the 2016 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
So self-assembly, shape-changing, and self-healing are starting to appear in labs as pieces. But their size, speed, and numbers are still far from the movie.
What about wearable robots? In 2013, U.S. Special Operations Command started TALOS, a so-called Iron Man suit combining armor and strength.
But in 2019, the program ended because it wasn't suited to close combat. Weight and power were the problems. Useful pieces, like lighter armor, were kept separately.
Factory exoskeletons appeared too. The U.S. company Sarcos said its exoskeleton could lift about 90 kilograms, but in 2023 the company dropped hardware and became an AI software company.
What actually spread in the workplace was simpler. Hyundai Motor Group's shoulder exoskeleton, unveiled in 2024, supports the arms with a mechanical structure and no motor or battery, reportedly cutting shoulder strain by up to 60 percent.
In medicine, in 2014, a wearable robot that helps people with paralyzed legs walk received its first FDA clearance in the U.S. This may be more amazing than Iron Man.
What about flying? A British jet suit actually lifts a person with five small jet engines on the arms and back.
Together the engines make over 1,000 horsepower. But because of fuel, one flight lasts only about three to four minutes.
In 2019, this suit set a Guinness record by flying at 136.9 kilometers per hour.
1,000 horsepower is about 746 kilowatts. If a fit adult working hard for an hour puts out 100 watts, that's the power of about 7,500 people at once.
Getting that power from batteries is even harder. By weight, jet fuel holds about 50 times more energy than the best lithium-ion batteries.
In the end, the nanosuit's real challenge is energy, not particles. A palm-sized arc reactor powering all this is the movie's biggest leap of imagination.
Finally, the person inside. However strong the suit is, a sudden high-speed takeoff sends that force straight into the blood and brain.
One science article pointed out that during a sudden takeoff, the suit might stop the legs from buckling, but the brain is another matter.
Even the G-suits worn by fighter pilots are said to protect only about 1 to 2 G. The rest the pilot has to endure through training.
Scoring it: self-assembling robots and self-healing materials have started in labs, wearable robots are partly in use, and energy and the human body are still big walls.
A complete movie-style nanosuit is still far away. But its pieces are already growing, one by one, in labs, factories, and hospitals.
